Network & Perimeter

Internal High-Volume Traffic & Port-Scan Investigation

Distinguishing malicious internal network reconnaissance from automated backup sweeps, vulnerability scanners, and repetitive infrastructure polling.

Traffic AnalysisAnomaly DetectionInternal NetworkPort ScanWireshark

Incident Scenario & Environmental Context

Network security telemetry flagged abnormal connection spikes across internal subnets. One host pair exhibited over 107,000 connection attempts between private IPv4 addresses (10.12.8.254 -> 10.12.7.114), while another internal node (10.32.116.175) recorded 3,913 connections to its gateway, with grouped telemetry indicating activity involving over 100 private IP addresses.

Investigation Workflow

1. Consolidated source and destination IP addresses, timestamps, and total connection volumes across the alert timeframe. 2. Compared connection counts to identify statistical anomalies between persistent high-volume sessions and widespread multi-host activity. 3. Distinguished point-to-point repetitive communication from multi-host horizontal sweeps (IP scanning) or multi-port vertical sweeps (port scanning). 4. Identified data-visibility limitations in summarized SIEM tables and pivoted to granular raw flow logs to enumerate target destination ports. 5. Analyzed TCP handshake flags (SYN-only vs established sessions vs RST packets) to measure connection success rates. 6. Cross-referenced the initiating private IP with enterprise CMDB asset inventories to verify if the node was an authorized vulnerability scanner or backup server.

Technical Analysis & Telemetry Insights

High connection volume across private IP subnets is a classic detection anomaly, but raw volume alone cannot distinguish malicious reconnaissance from routine enterprise operations. Modern enterprise networks frequently generate massive connection bursts due to legitimate infrastructure processes, including vulnerability assessment scanners (e.g., Qualys, Nessus), centralized backup agents (e.g., Veeam, Commvault) pulling VM snapshots, database replication jobs, and syslog collectors.

To isolate malicious port scans, the analyst must evaluate four dimensions: 1. Target Diversity: Does the source attempt connections to a wide range of unique destination IPs (horizontal scan / IP sweep) or cycle through hundreds of sequential TCP/UDP ports on a single host (vertical port scan)? 2. Timing and Distribution: Are connections spaced with randomized jitter (stealth scan) or concentrated in an aggressive multi-threaded flood? 3. TCP Flag Analysis: Malicious scanners frequently send SYN packets without completing the three-way handshake (SYN stealth scan), resulting in elevated RST (reset) responses from closed ports, whereas valid services negotiate full connections. 4. Data Visibility Constraints: High-level SIEM summary widgets often truncate connection records, hiding port scope. Pivoting to granular raw event logs is essential to confirm whether the target ports represent administrative services (SSH 22, RDP 3389, SMB 445) or ephemeral application sockets.

Diagnostic Query / Logic Sample

NetworkConnections | where TimeGenerated >= ago(4h) | where SourceIp in ("10.12.8.254", "10.32.116.175") | summarize ConnectionCount = count(), UniquePorts = dcount(DestinationPort), UniqueIPs = dcount(DestinationIp) by SourceIp, bin(TimeGenerated, 10m)

Competencies & Value Demonstrated

  • Recognition of unusual traffic volume and rapid baseline anomaly isolation
  • Grouping and contextual comparison of internal network communications
  • Identification of data-visibility limitations in summary dashboard widgets
  • Avoidance of unsupported conclusions when port-level telemetry is incomplete
  • Clear technical definition of next required evidence for triage closure

Incident Outcome & Verification Status

Verified Disposition

Traffic was classified as high-volume internal administrative synchronization following asset inventory verification. Confirmed malicious scanning was ruled out, and monitoring thresholds were fine-tuned.
